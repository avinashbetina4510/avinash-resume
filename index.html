Why a hospital takes the meeting

A platform sold to hospitals is bought on money it recovers. These are the two places that money sits, and I have measured both.

Discharge delay. A hospital knows its beds are full. It usually cannot say which step of the discharge chain is holding them. I broke discharge into twelve named steps and measured the bed-hours lost at each, then ranked the causes by cumulative impact so the top two or three account for most of the loss. That is the version of the problem a COO can act on. Around it sits a 41-metric catalogue with separate manager and ward-team views, discharge-before-noon benchmarks by ward and weekday, and a scorecard for how long each insurer takes to approve. Built at Apollo Hospitals as a summer project and carried into my dissertation.

Appointment no-shows. Every outpatient clinic loses slots to patients who never arrive, and most hospitals find a safe overbooking level by experimenting on real queues. I simulated the session instead. Patients arrive around their slot, a share do not arrive at all, and consultation times are drawn from a fitted distribution. The model measures what each overbooking level costs in waiting time against what it returns in recovered slots.

A clinic losing a fifth of its bookings leaves the doctor idle for roughly a third of the session.
